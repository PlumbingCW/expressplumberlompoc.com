Reverse Osmosis Drinking Water Filtration for Lompoc, CA Homes

Reverse osmosis cuts dissolved minerals at the kitchen tap. Lompoc's tap water is high in them, so here is what to expect.

By the Lompoc Express Plumbing teamPublished November 12, 2026Updated November 12, 2026

A reverse osmosis system pushes water through a membrane that blocks most dissolved solids. It sits under the kitchen sink and feeds a separate faucet for drinking and cooking. It lowers total dissolved solids, which is a good fit for water with a mineral taste, and it does not treat the rest of the house.

Lompoc's tap water meets state and federal standards and has a high mineral content. If you want a cleaner taste, an under-sink RO system is a common answer. Call (805) 249-7135 to talk through sizing and installation.

What reverse osmosis does and does not do

QuestionAnswer
Reduces dissolved minerals and saltsYes, substantially
Removes chlorine tasteUsually, through a carbon pre-filter
Softens water for the whole houseNo, it treats one tap
Wastes some waterYes, a portion goes to drain
Needs maintenanceYes, filters and the membrane need replacing on schedule
Lowers sodiumIt can reduce it at the drinking tap

RO is a point-of-use system. It treats the water at one faucet for drinking and cooking, which is a small fraction of your use. That keeps the cost and the wastewater modest.

Lompoc local note: the numbers behind the taste

The City of Lompoc's 2025 water quality report lists these city averages: total dissolved solids 817 ppm, sodium 134 ppm, hardness 293 ppm, sulfate 424 ppm and chlorine near 1.00 ppm. The report states that the water met all United States EPA and California drinking water health standards.

Those values explain the taste complaints. The standards are met, and the water is mineral-rich. An RO system lowers dissolved solids at the tap, and its carbon stage reduces chlorine. If you are on a sodium restricted diet, ask your doctor, because RO can lower sodium at the drinking tap but whole-house water would still carry it.

RO versus a softener versus a carbon filter

These do different jobs. A softener fixes scale and spotting everywhere. A whole-house carbon filter reduces chlorine for every tap. An RO fixes drinking water. Some homes use all three.

If the problem you notice is scale on faucets and dull glassware, a softener is the answer. If it is taste, RO or carbon will help. Our water softener and filtration page covers the sequence, and a whole-house carbon filter is the other common choice when taste and smell are the complaint.

What a typical installation involves

  1. Choose the location

    Under the kitchen sink, with room for the tank.

  2. Add the faucet

    A small drinking faucet is mounted on the sink or counter.

  3. Tap the supply and drain

    A cold water line feeds the unit and a drain saddle takes the waste.

  4. Flush and test

    New filters are flushed and the unit is tested for leaks.

If a countertop hole is needed, the installer will use the existing soap dispenser opening or drill a new one. If your kitchen is being remodeled, tell us, because it is easier to plan the system with the sink.

Maintenance, costs of ownership and waste

Pre-filters are generally replaced about once a year, and the membrane every few years, but the manufacturer's schedule governs. Skipping changes makes water taste worse and can damage the membrane.

Waste ratios vary by model. Efficient units reduce the wastewater, and a permeate pump helps. Ask about it when you choose. Cost of ownership includes filters over time, so compare the whole picture, not just the sticker.

Sizing the system for your kitchen

Most homes need a unit that fills a small storage tank overnight and delivers a glass of water on demand. Larger households or heavy coffee drinkers may want a higher capacity tank or a tankless RO unit with a pump. The right choice depends on usage and space.

Smell, taste and troubleshooting

If RO water tastes flat, that is normal because minerals are mostly gone. A remineralization stage can help. If it tastes stale, the tank may need sanitizing or the filters are overdue.

A slow dribble from the RO faucet can mean low tank pressure, a clogged pre-filter or a fouled membrane. A drain that gurgles loudly may need an air gap check. A plumber can test the tank pressure and swap parts in a short visit.

Keep a record of filter changes. Writing the date on the filter or on a sticker under the sink makes the schedule easy to follow.

Myths worth clearing up

  • RO does not make tap water unsafe or safe. It changes the mineral content and removes many dissolved substances.
  • RO water is not acidic enough to harm pipes in a point-of-use setup.
  • You do not need RO on every tap. The kitchen drinking faucet is the usual spot.
  • A bigger tank does not mean better water. It only stores more of it.
  • Installing RO does not remove the need for a softener if scale is the complaint.

Knowing what the system does and does not do keeps expectations realistic. For an honest recommendation, call and describe what you notice at the tap.

What to do before an RO install and when to call

  • Clear the cabinet under the sink and know where the cold valve is.
  • Do not cut the countertop yourself, because a wrong hole is expensive.
  • Call a plumber if you see a leak at a fitting after installation.
  • Change filters on the maker's schedule and write the date on the unit.
  • Do not drink from a system that has been out of service for weeks until it is flushed.

Who should consider an RO system

  • Households that notice a mineral taste in coffee or tea.
  • People who cook with a lot of tap water.
  • Anyone with a refrigerator ice maker or water line that clogs with scale.
  • People who want to reduce dissolved sodium at the drinking tap, with their doctor's advice.
