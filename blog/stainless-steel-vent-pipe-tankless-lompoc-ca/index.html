Stainless Steel Vent Pipe for Tankless Water Heaters in Lompoc, CA

Venting is where tankless installs most often go wrong. The vent material, size and routing come from the heater's manual, not from habit.

By the Lompoc Express Plumbing teamPublished November 12, 2026Updated November 12, 2026

The right vent pipe for a tankless water heater is the type the manufacturer lists for that exact model. Many condensing models allow PVC, CPVC or polypropylene. Non-condensing models usually require stainless steel, because their exhaust is hotter. Using the wrong material is a safety and warranty problem.

Do not guess from what is already on the roof. Call (805) 249-7135 and let a plumber check the model, the run length and the termination point before ordering parts.

Why vent material matters

Gas water heaters produce exhaust that must leave the house safely. Condensing tankless units extract extra heat, so the exhaust is cooler and wet, and it is acidic. Standard metal vent pipe would corrode. Non-condensing units produce hotter exhaust that plastic cannot take.

Vent materialTypical useNotes
Stainless steel (single or double wall)Non-condensing and some condensing unitsHeat and corrosion resistant, higher cost
PVC or CPVCMany condensing units onlyCheck the model's temperature rating
PolypropyleneMany condensing unitsSpecific to listed systems
Galvanized or B-ventNot for most tankless unitsCan fail quickly with condensate

Only the manufacturer's list matters. If the manual calls for stainless, it is not an option to downgrade.

Sizing, length and termination

DiameterTwo or three inches is typical, set by the model.
Maximum lengthEach elbow counts as extra length in the calculation.
SlopeCondensing vents must slope back toward the unit to drain.
TerminationDistance from windows, doors, vents and walkways is regulated.
SupportPipes must be supported at set intervals.
Combustion airDirect vent units pull air in through a second pipe.

A vent that is too long or has too many turns raises error codes and lowers efficiency. Your plumber should calculate the equivalent length before work starts.

Lompoc local note: coastal air and permits

Lompoc has a cool coastal climate. Fog is common, and the area sits near the ocean, so salt-laden air is part of daily life. Stainless steel resists corrosion well, but exposed fasteners and low-grade fittings can still stain. Use the grade the manufacturer lists and ask the installer about sealed terminations.

The City of Lompoc building division requires a permit before work on plumbing, gas and mechanical systems, including replacing systems. Venting is part of the mechanical and gas inspection, so plan for it. The California Plumbing Code, Title 24 Part 5, 2025 edition effective January 1, 2026, is the code the inspector applies.

Common vent mistakes

  • Reusing the old tank heater vent on a tankless unit.
  • Mixing parts from different manufacturers.
  • Missing slope on a condensing vent run.
  • Termination too close to a window or air intake.
  • Gaps at joints that are not sealed per the manual.
  • No condensate drain or neutralizer where one is required.

Any of these can cause error codes, nuisance shutdowns or a carbon monoxide risk. A carbon monoxide alarm near the sleeping area and the heater is inexpensive protection.

Condensate and drain details

Condensing tankless units release acidic condensate, usually a few cups per hour at full firing. That must drain to a suitable location, often through a neutralizer cartridge to protect the drain line. The neutralizer needs replacement on a schedule.

Plan the drain route early. A unit on an exterior wall may drain outdoors in some jurisdictions, and in others it must tie into a drain. Ask the building counter or your plumber which applies here.

Direct vent versus power vent

A direct vent unit uses sealed pipes, one for exhaust and one for intake, so it never draws air from the room. A power vent unit uses a fan to push exhaust out, and may use room air. The setup changes the pipe count, the routing and the space around the heater.

TypeAir sourceNotes
Direct ventOutside, through a second pipeCommon for sealed combustion and tight homes
Power ventRoom air in some modelsNeeds adequate combustion air
Outdoor unitOutside airShort or no vent pipe

Inspection and upkeep

Look at the vent run once a year. Check for loose joints, rust stains, drips at the elbows and sagging sections. Make sure the termination is clear of leaves, nests and snow or ice if it ever builds up.

If the display shows a vent-related error code, do not reset it repeatedly. The code means the unit sensed a problem, and the cause needs to be found. Turn the unit off and call a plumber if you smell exhaust.

What a vent quote should include

  • The vent material and diameter, named by the manufacturer's part type.
  • The total straight length and the number of elbows.
  • The roof or wall penetration, flashing and sealing details.
  • The condensate drain or neutralizer if the unit is condensing.
  • The permit and inspection fee, and who handles it.
  • Removal and capping of the old flue.

A quote that says only "vent kit" hides the details that cause problems later. Ask for the specifics in writing.

Replacing a tank heater with a tankless unit

Most tank heaters vent through a large draft hood into a masonry or B-vent flue. A tankless unit has different needs. The old flue may be capped, removed or relined, and a new vent is installed to the manufacturer's rules.

That change affects the roof, the attic and sometimes the neighboring appliances that share the flue. A furnace or boiler sharing the same flue can lose draft when the water heater is removed. A plumber must check this before the old vent is changed.

Plan for the extra work in the quote. It is part of doing the job safely.

  3. Plan the route
